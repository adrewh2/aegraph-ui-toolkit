# The explorer

**Unigraph** (`/unigraph`) is the graph explorer: a page that hosts explorer sessions as tabs — each with its own filter, layout and selection, split or side by side, like Entities; a new tab starts from the last-used filter. In a session, **2D** (`/unigraph/2d`) and **3D** (`/unigraph/3d`) are two cameras on the same picture, drawn by one engine (see [The engine](#the-engine)). Switching between them keeps the filter, the style, the selection and **every held position** — a layout is the same layout in both; the 3D view reads `z` (or 0) and the flat one ignores it. The addresses of the Graph Explorer that came before (`/graph/2d`, `/graph/3d`, `/graph/gpu`, `/graph/gpu3d`) open here, their query kept. Other pages stay open behind the one on screen for a quick return; Unigraph does not: leaving it — for another page, or another tab in its pane — lets its graph engine go, so a big project's picture holds no GPU memory or work while you are elsewhere, and coming back rebuilds it from what it keeps (the view's state, the camera, the held positions — a running simulation writes where it had got to on the way out).

The buttons top left of the toolbar — **Filter**, **Layout**, **Style**, then the statistics and the selection as icons — each open a panel flush against the left of the picture; one is open at a time, and pressing the open one's button closes it, so the picture has the whole width until you ask for a panel. Which panel was open is remembered. The three named ones are the stages a view goes through, in order: Filter → Layout → Style → Analysis (the statistics panel); the dataset comes before them (the open project, or an exported project loaded from the Export menu — a chip in the toolbar says which, with a way back) and Export after. See [The pipeline](/docs/pipeline) for why the order is fixed. Over MCP, `graph_viewport {panel}` opens a panel by name or closes it with `none`.

Beside the panel buttons stands the **scene**: the name of the one the view was loaded from (**Load scene** when none), with a dot while a part has drifted from it, and **Save** and **Save as…**. The name is a menu — every graph scene, searchable, the loaded one ticked; a click loads one, and hovering a row shows a card of what will load before it does: its picture, description, how many nodes and edges the view held after the filter, the filter, the layout and how many positions it holds, the style, the analysis, the dataset, the renderer, the view it keeps and when it was saved, with a **Load** button; **Unload** at the bottom forgets the scene and leaves the parts as they are. Save writes the view back into the loaded scene; Save as… names a new one. What is saved is a [scene](#scenes): an entity on the Scenes page (a *Unigraph scene*), holding the filter, the layout, the style and the analysis as saved parts of their own — parts not saved yet are saved with it, and the dialog says what happens to each — with the camera, the toggles, the selection and the panels as they are, and a small picture of the view. The view alone (a moved camera, a different selection) does not light the dot; it is captured again at the next save. The parts are entities too, rows under Entities → Graph; the explorer carries no save button for a part alone — the assistant's `graph_save {kind}` writes one — and a saved part opens into the explorer from its row (**Open in Unigraph**) or by link (`?filter=<id>`, `?layout=<id>`, `?style=<id>`, `?analysis=<id>`, `?scene=<id>`).

**Saved parts.** A filter, a layout and a style are each an entity you can save and load again, and the Filter, Layout and Style panels start with a strip in the scene's pattern: the part as a dropdown — the loaded one's name (or *Load filter* when none is), with a dot while it has changes the saved one has not — then **Save** (the disk) and **Save as…** (the disk with a plus), the only buttons. The dropdown lists the saved ones to load, the loaded one ticked, and holds **Rename…**, **Delete…** and the panel's reset (clear the filter, unload the layout so it is recomputed, the default style). What you change in the panel is a working copy: the saved entity is never written by an edit, only by **Save** — live while there are changes the saved one has not been given — and **Save as…** keeps the changes as a new entity, leaving the loaded one as it was. Loading another never asks about the changes: a filter, a layout or a style is cheap to make again, and the saved one is untouched. The **scene** picker in the toolbar bundles the three parts with the renderer and the analysis options — see [Scenes](#scenes).

## Filter

The filter decides *what is in view*: entity types, relationship types, tags (any / all), free text, a neighbourhood (roots + depth + direction), minimum/maximum degree, k-core, component size, giant component only, an exclude list, a node cap, and **field predicates** — comparisons on any [value](/docs/values) of a node (`data.year ≥ 2020`, `degree.in:cites > 0`, `meta.updatedAt` within the last week).

**Backbone** — a similarity mesh (every segment tied to the fifty most like it) draws as a ball of wool whatever the layout does, because most of its edges are noise at the scale you are looking at. The filter can keep only the edges that carry the structure before anything is drawn: *disparity* (Serrano, Boguñá & Vespignani's multiscale backbone — an edge survives where it is a larger share of a node's strength than chance would give, at significance α; needs weights, so name the field, e.g. `data.sim`), *simmelian* (each node keeps the `k` edges whose endpoints' neighbourhoods overlap most — the one for an unweighted nearest-neighbour graph), or *spanning* (the maximum spanning forest: every node kept, one skeleton, no cycles). Over MCP: `graph_filter {backbone, backbone_alpha, backbone_k, weight_field}`. On a 10k-node, 84k-edge atlas the simmelian backbone takes about 40 ms and leaves ~7k edges. Pair it with the **Tree (branches)** layout, which draws exactly the hierarchy that is left. A backbone is for a *mesh*; containment relationships are already a hierarchy, so there the relationship-type checklist is the whole job.

The panel keeps its shape while you filter, so the control you reach for next is where it was: a heading is one height whatever it holds, its **all** (put everything back) and the Tags **any of / all of** keep their places while they do not apply, *Min component size* stays (idle) while only the largest component is kept, and the foot of *Structure* is one line that is always there — how many nodes the filter matches and that all are drawn, with the components when those are counted, or, in amber, how many of them Max nodes lets through (the whole story on hover).

The filter runs over the dataset stage only. It never reads this iteration's analysis; to filter on PageRank, export the view as a project first (see [The pipeline](/docs/pipeline#iterating-with-projects)).

Saved filters are entities; loading one and editing it marks it *dirty* until `graph_save` writes it back.

**Script** — the filtering the panel cannot say, done by a saved [script](/docs/scripts): pick one and **Apply**, and the view keeps just the entities it returned (a collection, a list of ids, or a table with an `id` column) — the *Just these* section names the script, **Re-run** runs it again over the data as it stands, **Lift** lets everything back in. The other filters still apply on top, and a saved filter keeps the restriction and the script. A script applied here becomes a *filter script* (`script:filter`) if it had no kind yet; the Scripts page offers the same from under a result (**Keep these in Unigraph**), and the assistant's `graph_filter {script}` does it over MCP.

A table's **Graph** button opens Unigraph restricted to its rows (*From a table* in the filter panel, with a **Lift** to let the rest back in). With a relationship selected, **Table with source & target** puts that link and its two entities in a table, whose Graph button brings the same three back here — a round trip from any link to a table of what it connects and back to a picture of just that.

Every row of the type, relationship-type and tag checklists counts **x of y**: x in view after the filter, y in the whole project (the dataset the explorer draws from, a sub-project when the Dataset stage is one) — *37 of 37* with nothing filtered, *0 of 37* for a type unticked, *480 of 543* for the relationships that lose an end to it; a tag namespace counts the entities carrying any of its tags. The checklists are histograms too: each row is shaded, in its own colour, to its count in view against the largest in its list — a type's colour being the one the picture draws it in, the legend's, so a style that colours by type changes the list with it — so the panel reads the shape of the picture at a glance — a checked row's bar is full-strength, an unchecked row's faint.

Tags sit beside types as a checklist of their own, not a second-class chip box: every tag is checked until you uncheck one, and unchecking hides the nodes that carry it; **only** flips to an include list (a node must carry any — or all — of the checked tags), and both apply together with the type and relationship-type checklists. Namespaced tags (`tier:app`, `tier:platform`) fold under their prefix with a count of the nodes they touch, so hundreds of tags stay one screen; the prefix row is a switch for the whole namespace (click to hide or show everything tagged `domain:…`, double-click or **only** for just those), its checkbox half-filled when only some of its tags are checked, and the chevron opens the tags underneath. Every section of the panel folds to its heading (the chevron), and what is folded is remembered. The legend's title is a picker: **Type**, or any tag namespace ("Tag · tier", "Tag · severity" — the part before the colon, with each value a colour), or the first tag — switching it recolours the graph and the legend follows; on a type or tag legend each row is also a switch — click it to hide or show those nodes, double-click for only those (again to bring the rest back), the same rule the Style tab's eye button sets, so the two agree — overriding any per-type or per-tag colour the style had set (their size and shape rules stay). Nodes without such a tag are grey and counted as *none*. The legend lists every value — every type, every value of the namespace, most frequent first — and scrolls, with the sidebar's thin scrollbar, when there are more than its height shows; it ends above the view's controls. Where the colours come from a palette (a field, a metric), values past the palette's colours share them, and a line under the list says after how many; a tag or a type keeps a colour of its own. Past 500 values the rest are counted as *N more*. The Style tab's *Group by* offers the same choices with the full palette controls.

### Large graphs

The explorer draws up to a **view budget** — two million nodes and ten million edges, what the engine is built for — without asking. A filter that would draw more is held back: instead of the canvas you get the **Large graph** gate, a breakdown of what the view would hold by node type and relationship type, with the most numerous relationship kinds already unticked (similarity and co-occurrence links are usually the bulk and the least readable) and a node cap (the filter's own **Max nodes**, two million unless you set it — a safety net, not a budget). *Show selection* applies that as a filter change — nothing leaves the data, it is just not drawn; *Show everything* draws the view as is.

The gate also comes up, under the heading **Too large for this tab**, when drawing the view would take more memory than the tab can spare — whatever its size against the budget. A browser gives each tab a fixed amount of memory (about 4 GB in Chrome), and a big project already holds much of it once open: the WildChat atlas demo holds about 1.5 GB, and drawing all of it with its filter cleared — a million nodes and a million edges — takes the tab to about 3.3 GB at its peak, close enough to the limit that a tab with a little more in it is stopped by the browser. So the explorer measures the tab's memory (Chrome reports it; elsewhere it assumes half a million nodes and two million edges) and, for any view over 50,000 nodes or 200,000 edges, asks first when the drawing's peak would bring the tab past 70% of its limit. The gate says how much the drawing needs and how much the tab can spare, and its suggested selection is sized to fit. *Show everything* takes a second click there, because it can close the tab and lose what is not saved; the choice lasts until the page is reloaded. A view you accepted is not asked about again while it stays about that size. Raising Max nodes afterwards just draws more of the view you chose — it is not asked about again. A graph opened from Insights arrives with its cap set and its size accepted. The budget itself is editable at the bottom of the gate, and over MCP `graph_filter` takes `accept_large` and `view_budget` while `graph_state.load` reports the size, the budget, whether the gate is up and why (`reason`: `budget` or `memory`).

## The engine

Unigraph's picture — the filter's nodes and edges at their positions, in the style, with the selection, the path and the pins — is drawn by one engine built for graphs of hundreds of thousands to a million nodes: WebGPU where the browser has it, WebGL2 otherwise (the badge at the bottom left says which). The **2D** and **3D** tabs are its flat camera and its orbit camera over the same data, so switching keeps everything in place. It draws what the style says: colour, size, opacity and visibility, the four node shapes (circle, square, diamond, triangle), edge width and arrows, curved parallel edges when the style asks for curves, and edge labels when it asks for those.

What makes it fast is not one trick but a rule: the cost of a frame follows what is on screen, the cost of an interaction follows the interaction, and nothing follows the size of the graph. The graph lives in packed typed arrays — integer indices, a CSR adjacency, bitsets for what is visible, selected and around the selection — and the GPU holds a copy that is patched, never rebuilt: moving a node uploads one node, selecting a thousand uploads a thousand flags, and a frame is three or four draw calls whatever the size. Picking goes through a spatial grid in 2D and a GPU id pass in 3D; a marquee, a neighbourhood or a select-all is a bitset operation. Layouts are the explorer's own, from the Layout tab; two are Unigraph's: **Multilevel (communities)** and **Force (Unigraph)** (see [Layout](#layout)). A big view that arrives with no positions and the ForceAtlas2 layout selected would wait minutes, so the engine switches the algorithm to Multilevel and runs it — a picture in a second or two, held and saveable like any other layout; pick Force (Unigraph) afterwards to relax it on the GPU, tuning its parameters as it runs. An explorer kept behind another page or tab waits before starting a layout or an automatic analysis: one view, one worker.

**Physics** is a live force simulation over the whole picture — a million nodes and two and a half million edges at a few milliseconds an iteration — computed on the GPU where the browser has WebGPU (the positions step in place, so a frame costs nothing extra to show) and in the engine's worker otherwise. The model: every node repels every other through a grid pyramid — a grid that moves a little every iteration, so its cells do not print themselves on a dense cluster as rows and columns — (the dimension's Coulomb law, so a uniform ball's repulsion cancels a pull to the centre and the picture packs at the link length instead of ballooning with its size), links pull toward a rest length weighted by 1/√degree so hubs do not fold everything onto them. Up close a node is pushed by the nodes of its own cell one by one — a sample of them where a cell is crowded, different every iteration — and each link's rest length and strength differ a little, by a hash of its two ends: so a hub's thousands of leaves settle as a cloud that thins toward its edge, where identical springs and nodes that never pushed their cell-mates packed them in rings around the hub or spokes out of it. A *Run* cools d3-style and stops, with a layout to hand back. The defaults were chosen by measuring the pictures — how well distances on screen follow distances in the graph, how many edges cross, whether neighbours sit together, whether nodes land on top of each other — on grids, trees, communities, hub-heavy graphs and real data, against ForceAtlas2: full-strength links and a weak gravity (so a structure unfolds — a grid comes out a grid), repulsion softened only in the last tenth of a link (so nodes do not settle on each other), and a run of about 1,500 iterations before it cools. On the GPU that is a second or two for a small graph: a handful of iterations a frame while the picture moves a lot, as many as the frame's budget allows once the moves are small. A big graph needs more to unfold, so it gets more — up to three times as many from about two hundred thousand nodes — as many as fit about eight seconds. On the CPU (the worker, and a Run on a tab without WebGPU) iterations are costlier: a run on a big graph is cut to a few hundred, cooling faster to fit them, so it still ends settled. A node the simulation meets somewhere impossible — no number for a position — starts again near the centre instead of spreading to the rest; **Physics** is the same simulation left switched on — it cools and the picture comes to rest, and a drag, a parameter change or a change to the graph warms it back up so it settles again around what changed, until you switch it off. A *Run* while it is on borrows the simulation and gives it back: the run settles, its positions are held as the layout, and the switch stays on over the picture at rest. While it runs, the positions are the engine's: nothing is copied anywhere for the sake of it. While it moves, what it has reached is written to storage about once a second — straight out of the engine as numbers, which is a few milliseconds even at eight hundred thousand nodes — so a reload lands on the picture as it was — read from storage while the Library itself loads, so at a million nodes it is there a second after the view is, not seconds later — and the session's own copy is brought up to date at the moments that need it: when the run stops, when you leave the view, and whenever anything asks for the positions (a save, an export, a scene, a tool). Meanwhile the project can change underneath it — an entity added or removed, by you or by the assistant — and the picture takes the change without losing its place: what arrives is placed beside the picture for the simulation to take up, and a node you move yourself, or a tool moves, goes where you put it. A drag reheats it; pinned nodes hold — and a node you put somewhere while it runs is pinned there (unpin it to let the springs have it back). A press while it runs waits the frame or two it takes to read the positions back from the GPU, so the node under the pointer is the one you get, however fast it moves. Its kernels compile in the background as soon as the engine starts, so switching it on does not stall the page, and handing a million positions back when it settles is a few milliseconds. A view behind another is paused, simulation and all: no frames, no iterations, and no hand-backs — it gives the positions it reached over once on the way behind, and the page you moved to has the machine to itself. Switching it on keeps the picture's extent — the rest length is the spacing the nodes would have spread evenly over the ball they occupy — and, when it settles or is switched off, the positions it reached are held like any layout's, so they save — except where you put nodes yourself while it ran: a placement (every tree where it stands), a transform, a loaded layout or a dragged node is newer than the simulation and stays; switching the physics off never puts a ball back over a picture you arranged. `repulsion`, `gravity` (less of it, a looser picture), `link_distance`, `link_strength`, `damping`, `alpha_decay`, `max_step`, `link_jitter` and `spring_jitter` are its parameters over MCP (`gpu_graph physics`).

**Semantic zoom** is part of the renderer, not a cosmetic: what a node gets depends on the room it has on screen. Far out, nodes are dots and edges straight lines with no labels — the picture is density, and the links are part of it: drawn strongly enough to see which clusters are joined and how much, and past half a million in view a sample of them, each drawn as opaque as the edges it stands for. Closer, nodes take their styled size, edges become anti-aliased lines, and only the focus (hover, selection, path) is labelled. Closer still, arrowheads and a density-limited set of labels appear; at reading distance everything in view is labelled, one label per patch of screen so they never pile up. The **Labels** control (auto / all / none) still applies on top.

Interaction: click to select, ⇧ or ⌘ to add, ⇧-drag a marquee, drag a node (or the whole selection it belongs to), wheel to zoom, right-click for the menu; in 3D, drag orbits and a **right-button drag** translates the view — the pan 2D has on a plain drag (⌥-drag and the middle button pan too); a right button released without moving is the menu, as everywhere; the wheel zooms about the pointer, as in 2D — the camera closes on what is under it, which stays under it — and double-clicking a node zooms to it.

**Benchmark** — at the top left of the picture, shown when **Developer tools** is on in the settings menu at the foot of the sidebar — loads a generated graph of a chosen size — 10k, 50k, 100k, 200k, 500k or 1m nodes, with 2.5 edges per node — in place of the explorer's picture, or runs the suite on one: generate, load, first frame, camera frames while panning and zooming (p50/p95), picking, select-all and clear, a neighbourhood, dragging one node and a thousand, a marquee, a filter, the coarse layout, all in milliseconds. Generated graphs never enter the entity system; **← Explorer graph** brings the explorer's picture back. On an Apple laptop the 1m preset (a million nodes, two and a half million edges) loads in under a second, draws a frame in about 10 ms of GPU time and pans and zooms at well under a millisecond of CPU per frame; selecting everything takes about 10 ms and a filter about 50. Far out, where a million edges land on a few hundred thousand pixels, the renderer draws a sample of them (every second to eighth edge) as many times as opaque — the same density for a fraction of the work; every edge is back as soon as the view is close enough to tell.

Over MCP: the explorer's tools work on this tab as on the others (`graph_state`, `graph_filter`, `graph_layout`, `graph_style`, `graph_select`, `graph_viewport`… take `mode: gpu` or `gpu3d`), and `gpu_graph` (its `open` shows Unigraph) reaches the engine itself — its backend, counts and frame times, the benchmark, its layouts, the physics (`on`, `params`, `iterations` to run and wait, `reheat`), and selection, filter, camera and moves by index.

## Layout

Two ways to get positions:

- **Algorithms** — the picker lists them in two groups: **dynamic** layouts are simulations that can be left running (the *Keep running* switch under *Simulation*, the same switch as **Physics** in the toolbar: the picture keeps settling, reacts to drags and pins, and the positions it reaches are held), **static** ones compute once. **Force (Unigraph)** comes first and is the default: the one the engine runs live, at any size, and in a layout-quality benchmark as good as ForceAtlas2 or better on most graphs. ForceAtlas2 (d3-force in 3D) is a simulation too, but stepped in the background for the iterations given, and only up to 25,000 nodes. A view that was on ForceAtlas2 only because it used to be the default moves to Force (Unigraph) once; picking ForceAtlas2 again sticks. The layouts: force-directed (ForceAtlas2), radial by type, clusters by type, circle pack, concentric by degree, layered hierarchy (dagre), circular, grid, sphere (3D), random, **Multilevel (communities)** — communities within communities, drawn as balls within balls (discs within discs in 2D): the graph's Louvain hierarchy, its top level laid out whole, then each level placed inside its parent — hubs and big sub-communities in the middle, nodes with neighbours elsewhere on the side facing them — and settled by a few rounds of collisions and springs, with a wall at the parent's edge so no community spills into the next; components not joined to the rest, and every node with no edge at all, share one ball beside the picture. A weight field, when set, weighs the communities. Linear per level with nothing quadratic, it lays out 800,000 nodes in a few seconds; the choice for a big view without WebGPU — and **Force (Unigraph)**, Unigraph's own force model: repulsion summed through a cell pyramid (nothing quadratic), springs at a **link distance** weighted against hubs, a weak **gravity** that sets the density, **damping** and **cooling** in the d3 manner. Its knobs sit under Parameters; *Run* steps it on the GPU (a million nodes at a few milliseconds an iteration) from the picture as it stands — or from a multilevel seed when nodes have no position — until it cools or *Iterations* are spent, and the knobs apply **while it runs**, so a picture can be tuned by eye; the **Physics** switch is the same simulation left running (without WebGPU the same model steps in the worker). **A big graph with no positions** — more than two thousand nodes waiting for a layout — gets Force (Unigraph) on its own when the browser has WebGPU (a multilevel seed, then the simulation on the GPU), and Multilevel without WebGPU, rather than the ForceAtlas2 default, which would take minutes at that size; the Layout tab shows the choice, and any other algorithm can be picked after. Over MCP: `graph_layout {algorithm: "unigraph-force", unigraph: {link_distance, link_strength, repulsion, gravity, damping, cooling}}`. Parameters live in the Layout tab; *Recompute* runs the algorithm again. A force layout over more than a few hundred nodes is computed in the background: the view shows the nodes at seed positions under a *Laying out…* notice, stays responsive, and frames the result when it lands (`graph_state` reports it as `layoutRunning`).
- **Engines fetched on demand** — heavier layouts that are not part of the app until you pick one; each is its own download, made the first time it runs, and every one computes in the layout worker: **Layered (ELK)**, **Stress (ELK)**, **Tree (ELK)** and **Radial (ELK)** from the Eclipse Layout Kernel (the engine, 1.4 MB, runs in a worker of its own); **Constraint (WebCola)** in 2D and 3D — stress majorization with overlap avoidance; **Force (ngraph)** in 2D and 3D — n-body springs over a Barnes–Hut tree, quick on big graphs; and **Spectral** in 2D and 3D — coordinates from the graph Laplacian, deterministic and dependency-free. They take the same parameters (spacing, iterations, direction where it applies) and respect pinned nodes.
- **How long they take** — measured headlessly (M-series Mac, software GL, other work running) on the Aurora demo drawn whole, 5,960 nodes and 14,094 edges, every layout in the worker with the page responsive throughout: spectral 0.6 s (2D) / 1.7 s (3D); force 17–25 s (2D) / 29 s (3D); ngraph 18 s (2D) / 34 s (3D); WebCola past two minutes at this size (its cost grows with the square of the node count — 10 s at 800 nodes, where ngraph takes 0.7 s and spectral 0.35 s); radial by type under half a second. ELK layered and stress refuse more than 3,000 nodes / 20,000 edges with a sentence rather than hang (ELK layered takes 0.3 s at 1,500 nodes / 16k edges, 2 s at 800 nodes / 2,800 edges); the built-in layered (dagre) runs past a minute at this size — keep those to a few hundred nodes. First use of an engine costs its download once per browser: ELK's 1.4 MB worker adds about 0.2 s, ngraph and WebCola under 0.1 s. *Overlap removal* after a layout runs in the worker too once the view passes 800 nodes, whatever the algorithm, and is skipped when the picture piles more than a thousand nodes into one cell of its grid (a spectral layout's centre): it would take tens of seconds and then fail. The panel warns above each layout's comfortable range, and some layouts have a hard limit where, measured on sparse graphs of communities, a run would pass twenty seconds and go on for minutes: layered (dagre) 300 nodes, Stress (ELK) 400, Tree (ELK) 700, WebCola 1,200, Layered (ELK) 1,300, Radial (ELK) 6,000, ngraph 20,000, ForceAtlas2 25,000, Spectral 80,000. Past its limit a layout is greyed out in the picker (with the limit beside it), *Run* is off with the reason, `graph_layout` refuses it, and a view that grew past the limit of its layout — or a scene or a session that names one — is laid out by Force (Unigraph) where the GPU runs it, Multilevel otherwise. Force (Unigraph), Multilevel, Tree, Circle pack and the arrangements by type have no limit: they scale, or (Force (Unigraph) on the CPU) keep to a few seconds of work by taking fewer iterations on a big graph. Over MCP, `graph_layout` waits 15 s and then reports `layoutRunning: true` rather than holding the bridge.
- **Placement** — put a selection of nodes somewhere from their values: a grid, a scatter (a heatmap grid when binned), a circle, a line, or stacks by a category, anchored to a region of the current viewport. See [Position layouts](/docs/positions).

- **Custom positions** — the first entry of the picker, and the one selected whenever the picture's positions were *set* rather than computed: a placement, positions set by hand or by a tool (`graph_positions set` / `transform`), or a loaded layout that came from one of those (the layout carries its placement rule, or says `manual`). It names what the picture is instead of an algorithm that would draw something else; nothing recomputes it — **Run** places only nodes that have no position, on a grid beside the picture — and choosing an algorithm then pressing **Recompute** lays it all out again. Drags never change the selected layout: a dragged force layout is still a force layout.

A position you set while a layout is still running — a placement, a transform, a drag — is newer than the run's result and stays when it lands, the same way the live physics leaves what you arranged alone. A **placement** (every tree where it stands, a grid by year — the Layout tab's *Place*, or `graph_positions place`) is a fixed arrangement and stays one: while its rule is in force, choosing another algorithm or pressing Run lays out only the nodes it did not place, and the automatic switches (a big view moving to Multilevel) do the same; **Recompute** forgets the rule and lays everything out again, as does clearing it. Whatever produced them, positions are **held**: dragging a node, running an algorithm or placing nodes writes into one position map that the renderers read. *Pinned* nodes keep their place during force layouts. Held positions survive a reload whatever their number (a big view's go to the browser's IndexedDB rather than localStorage) and are not recomputed when the page comes back — the picture is where you left it, and only nodes without a position get placed; **Save** turns them into a layout entity (with the placement rule, if there was one) that any view can load later.

## Style

**A type's colour.** A type is drawn in the colour it declares — set it on its page in [Ontology], or with `define_entity_type` — and a type that declares none is given one: the same colour on every reload of that project, on any machine, and never another type's. The colours are picked off a grid of hues, each a golden angle from the last, crossed with three bands of lightness; the types with no colour of their own are spread over that grid by name, so two of them never come out alike. (This is the ontology's set, not the view's: filtering to a handful of types recolours nothing.) Tags work the same way, by tag name.

Defaults first, then overrides. **Defaults** are one short row per channel — nodes: colour, size, opacity, shape, labels; edges: colour, width, opacity, arrows — and every element follows them unless a row below says otherwise. Each channel is a **constant** (set right on the row: a swatch, a number, a shape), a **ranking** (a numeric value mapped onto a palette or a min–max range, linear or log) or a **partition** (a categorical value mapped onto a categorical palette); open the row to change the mode or the mapping. Values come from the same grammar as everywhere else: degree, a data field, a metric, the type, a tag, or any [value reference](/docs/values).

A **style script** can decide every node's look in code — `ds.compute(['pagerank', 'community'])` for the metrics, `style(nodes, { legend })` for the result (see [Scripts](/docs/scripts)); applied, it becomes a saved style whose per-node styles ride on top of the rows here, with the script's legend. The panel names the script, runs it again, or drops the scripted styles. The same goes for a **layout script** (`layout(positions)`) in the Layout panel, and a **filter script** in the Filter panel; a saved scene keeps all three by their entities, so it reopens without running anything.

**Overrides** are rows you add with **+**, one per type or tag in view. A row names only the channels it sets — the rest inherit the default — and shows just those; open it to override another channel or send one back to inheriting. The eye hides everything in the row's scope (hidden always wins), × removes the row. **Options** (folded) hold precedence — when a node matches both a type row and a tag row, whose channels apply — edge labels and curved edges. Saved styles are entities; the legend follows whatever colour partition is in force.

## The detail pane

The panel on the right is always there. Its first tabs, **Nodes** and **Edges**, are everything in view as tables — search, sort and filter them like any Entities table, but read-only. A click selects the row at once (the **Selected** tab follows it) and flies the camera to it without changing the zoom — zooming out when the picked rows would not fit, and in when the pick would be a dot at the zoom you are at (the view many times wider than a neighbourhood around it), so on a big graph you land on the node, not a pixel; a double-click opens the row's entity as a tab of its own as well. Picking an edge frames its two ends. Clicking a node on the canvas opens the **Selected** tab; anything opened from inside a detail view — a conversation behind a segment, say — becomes another tab, and **+** finds any entity to open here. Every tab has a close button; the pane's own button folds it to a strip at the edge, and a new selection unfolds it.

## Selection and tools

- Click selects; shift/⌘-click adds; drag on empty space draws a marquee. The **selection panel** (the pointer icon among the panel buttons) starts with the selection: while nothing is selected it says how to select, and its two rows of actions — *Select* all, invert, none; *Add* neighbours, same type, same tag — stay where they are, idle until there is something to act on. Under it: what a click does (*Select* or *Shortest path*, with the path's options), the **highlight** — how many links out from the selection stay lit while the rest of the picture dims (1–6; `graph_selection_action {highlight_hops}`; a hover lights one ring) — the pinned nodes, and whether the camera glides or jumps (it always jumps when your system asks for reduced motion — [Getting around](/docs/navigation#keys)).
- **Right-click** is where a node's or a relationship's actions are, in 2D and 3D alike. The graph's own come first: open, select, select neighbours, focus on the neighbourhood, path from here, add the linked entities the filter leaves out, open in a new graph, name the community (once communities are computed), pin, tag, hide, download as `.ae.json`. Then the menu every entity has, the same as a table row's ([Tables](/docs/navigation#tables)): rename, edit, annotate, link to, related entities, ask the assistant, chart, set property, projects, copy the id or JSON, delete. On a node inside the selection the menu acts on the whole selection. A relationship's menu opens it, selects it or both ends, puts the ends in a new graph, and then has the same entity menu (its related entities are the link with its ends). Right-clicking empty space offers fit, select all / invert / clear, the path (select it, clear it) and unhide.
- **Edges are clickable**: a click a few pixels from a line selects the relationship and opens it in the detail pane, in every renderer; a right-click there is its menu.
- The wheel zooms; the view's own controls sit **bottom right of the picture**: the pointer tool (select, or the **path** tool, which picks two nodes and shows the shortest path — directed and weighted options are in the Selection tab), **zoom in**, **zoom out**, **Fit to view** (the frame) and **Full view** (the arrows): full view closes the side panel and folds the app's sidebar to its icon rail with it, so the picture has the width; the same button brings the panel back — the one that was open — and the sidebar too when full view was what folded it (a sidebar you had already folded stays folded). Opening a panel from its toolbar button leaves full view the same way. The details panel and the assistant are yours: full view leaves them as they are. The choice is remembered. Fit is also on the right-click menu on empty space, and over MCP `graph_viewport` with `full: true | false` does the same as Full view. While the path tool is at work, a line at the bottom says what to click next and what was found.
- **The picture holds still.** Only you move it: when something opens or closes beside it — the details panel, the assistant, the sidebar folding to its rail, a pane gutter dragged — every node stays under the pixel of the screen it was under, at the same scale, and the picture gains or loses a strip at the edge that moved. In 3D too, at every depth: the camera does not move, the new view is the old one cropped or extended, and the next fit (or a camera set over MCP) starts from a centred projection again. Only *Full view* refits, because that is what it is for. A **filter** changed — narrowed, widened, a type ticked off — keeps the camera where you have it, too: the nodes that stay are where they were on screen. Only when nothing of the new picture would be in view (a filter down to things elsewhere) is it framed, so you are never left looking at an empty canvas; a scene, a different layout or 2D ↔ 3D frames the picture as it arrives. Nor does the picture arrive in two steps: on a refresh the canvas is shown only once its first camera is in place — the one remembered for that picture, or the scene's when a scene is being loaded — so nothing is drawn framed one way and then jumped to another.
- Every move the app makes for you in 2D — to a row picked in the pane, a tab clicked as a bookmark, a search result, a fit, a zoom step, `graph_viewport` — flies the same path: a short hop is a flick, and a long one pulls back on the way so the place it left and the place it is going share the screen, then settles in (the zoom-and-pan of van Wijk and Nuij, as in d3). It takes between a third of a second and just under one, by the distance; a drag or the wheel takes the camera back at once.
- **Hops** lights up the neighbourhood of the selection.
- Keyboard: `F` fits, `⌘A` selects all, `I` inverts, `N` adds the selection's neighbours, `P` pins or unpins, `H` hides, `⌫` deletes (with confirmation), `Esc` clears — the path first, then the selection.
- **Camera keys**, held down for as long as you want the camera to move. In 3D there are two sets, **Orbit** and **Fly**, switched at the left end of the faint key line at the bottom right of the picture (it remembers your choice; Orbit to begin with). The line lists the keys of the set that is on, and it never takes a click but for the switch.
  - **Orbit** — the camera moves around the point it looks at. The **arrow keys** slide it across the picture — in 2D over the plane, in 3D sideways and up and down in the plane facing the camera — the zoom and the angle kept; two at once go diagonally, at a share of the view per second (about 60%), so it feels the same close in and far out. **Q** zooms in and **E** zooms out, about the centre of the view (in 3D the camera closes on the point it looks at, and slows as it nears it — it never passes it). In 3D, **W A S D** turn the camera around that point: **A** and **D** go around to the left and right, **W** rises over the top and **S** drops underneath, stopping short of straight above or below. A drag orbits too, and the wheel zooms about the point under the pointer, which stays where it is on the screen, in 3D as in 2D. 2D has only this set.
  - **Fly** (3D) — the camera moves itself, with the point it looks at carried along, so it goes into the graph, through it and out the other side at a steady pace. **W** and **S** move forward and back along the line of sight, **A** and **D** sideways, **Q** and **E** down and up; the **arrow keys** turn the view about the camera — left and right, up and down — so past the graph a turn faces back at it. A drag looks around from where the camera is; the wheel moves it toward what the pointer is over and back (straight ahead at the centre of the view), and a pinch and the zoom buttons forward and back.

  Keys combine — a slide while zooming, a turn while rising — and `Shift` makes any of them three times as fast. They go by the key's place on the keyboard, not the letter, so they sit in the same spot on any layout. A field, a menu or a dialog keeps its own keys. Over MCP, `graph_viewport` makes the same moves: `{action: "pan", by: {x, y}}` in widths and heights of the view, `{action: "zoom", factor}`, and in 3D `{action: "orbit", yaw, pitch}` in degrees, `{action: "fly", move: {forward, right, up}}` in shares of the graph's size and `{action: "look", yaw, pitch}`; `controls: "orbit" | "fly"` flips the switch.
- Selection actions — tag / untag, hide / unhide, pin / unpin, focus (restrict the view to the neighbourhood), **Related** (the Entities table on everything linked to the selection — the linked entities and the relationships themselves, with the filter shown as a removable chip), delete, export — are on the right-click menu and in the Selection tab. *Related entities* is on every right-click menu in the app: table rows, plot points, and the selection here.

## Stats

The **statistics panel** (the chart icon) measures the current view — degree variants, PageRank, betweenness, closeness, eigenvector, HITS, clustering coefficient, components, Louvain communities, plus density, diameter and average path length — off the main thread. It reads top to bottom in the order it is used: the run bar (*8 of 14 measures* opens the list to choose from, grouped as Connections, Importance, Structure and Communities, each measure with what it means; **Run**), then **The view** in a few figures (links per node, density, pieces, communities, clustering, widest distance), then **Per node**: one row per measure with its range, and two buttons — colour the picture by it, size the nodes by it — lit while the style uses it; a row opens to its distribution, its meaning and the highest-scoring nodes (a click selects one). The list of measures folds away once there are results. **Keep the results** saves them as an analysis entity or writes them onto the entities (`data.<prefix><metric>`, the prefix asked for in the dialog). *Keep up to date as the view changes* re-runs them when the filter changes what is in view; when the view changed and they were not re-run, the panel says so. See [Analysis](/docs/analysis).

## Scenes

A **graph scene** (type `scene`, a subtype of the Scenes page's scene — it is listed there too, and opens here) is the whole arrangement in one entity: the view (2D or 3D), filter, layout, style, the analysis when metrics were computed (saved as an entity like the other parts), project, analysis options — and everything about the view and the UI besides, so loading it puts the screen back as it was: the camera, the labels, edges and legend toggles, the selection and the selected edge, hops, the tool and its path options, which side panel was open and how wide, the details pane (folded, maximised, which tab, which entities pinned). Physics is recorded but loads off — a running simulation would move the picture away from the one saved; start it again and it continues from these positions. Each scene keeps a small picture of the view as it was saved and how many nodes and edges it held: the card's image and figures on the Scenes page and in the explorer's scene menu, so you see what you will load. A scene composed with no screen — by the headless engine's `graph_scene`, a generator, a demo bundle — has no view to capture, and a picture drawn from what it holds can only be a sketch: the nodes at their saved positions over their relationships, flat and from above, with no camera, no style and no third dimension. **Open it once and its card becomes the picture it opens as**: the view captures its own frame when the scene has settled and keeps it in this browser (an IndexedDB store of its own), keyed to the scene and the moment it last changed, so an edited scene never shows the old picture. It is kept there rather than on the entity because opening a scene is not a change to the project — nothing is written back, and a bundle carries only the picture that was saved with it. Its type is `scene:unigraph`; the toolbar's Save and Save as… write it and capture the view, the counts and the picture (so does `graph_save {kind: 'scene'}`, with `update: true` for Save). Scenes saved before — the bare `scene`, which the templates ship, and `scene:explorer`, saved on the Graph Explorer that Unigraph replaced — read the same and take the type when next saved; a scene that named that page's renderer (sigma or three.js, or the engine's GPU tab) opens in the dimensions it had. Loading one restores the view; saving one saves any unsaved parts along the way. Saving while physics runs keeps the picture on screen — the layout holds the positions the simulation has reached — so loading the scene later shows exactly that, with physics off, and starting physics continues from there (`npm run smoke:scenes` checks this round trip over the bridge). Scenes are what workstation tabs and links (`/unigraph/2d?scene=<id>`) point at.

Scenes and their parts live under Entities → Graph. Each row's context menu has **Open in Unigraph · 2D / 3D**, and the detail view's first tab, **Graph**, shows what the entity holds (a scene lists its parts, each a link) with the same two buttons. A part opens alone — `?filter=<id>`, `?layout=<id>`, `?style=<id>`, `?analysis=<id>` — into whatever the explorer is showing, leaving the rest as it is; a scene replaces the whole arrangement. **`?physics=1`** starts the live simulation once the view is up (`?physics=0` stops it): a scene loads with physics off, so a tab that wants the picture alive — a demo project's opening view — says so in its address. **`?fresh=1`** starts the tab from the explorer's defaults instead of the last-used tab's filter, project, style and layout: the whole open project, unfiltered (with `?only=`, it clears the rest of the filter around the pick). The first visit's tab is `/unigraph/3d?physics=1&fresh=1`. Both parameters are spent once the view is up, so a reload keeps what was done since. Nothing else lays the view out while a simulation is running it: the seed cloud is the simulation's to work on, and the camera is framed again as the picture takes shape. A view opened on a scene computes nothing until the scene is in either: what the tab held before it — the whole project, on a fresh one — is about to be replaced by the scene's own filter and its own saved positions, so no layout is run over it.

## Slides

A web of thousands of facts, documents and people is too much to read at once; a **slide** is the human-sized page someone makes out of it. A slide (type `slide`) has a title, an optional kicker above it and a few lines of text (`- ` bullets, `**bold**`), and it **shows** some entities — a `shows` relationship from the slide to each. A **deck** (type `deck`) is an ordered set of slides with a colour. Both are project-category types, so the Dataset stage leaves them out of the picture: the web is drawn as it is, whatever slides are made of it.

In **3D** the slides of the decks shown hang over the web at fixed places — a ring above it, each card facing out, a deck reading left to right from outside, the decks one after another round the ring, stacked on tiers once there are more slides than one ring holds (a deck never splits between tiers) — and from each card a beam falls to every node it shows. Orbit and they stay where they are: you walk round them, and one seen from behind is drawn faded and mirrored back so it still reads. They are placed by the web's own size — its centre, cards sized by the distance nine in ten of its nodes lie within, the ring clear of all but its outermost few — so a re-run layout keeps them over it; a slide with a `plane` of its own hangs exactly there instead (in radii of the web from its centre, turned by `yaw`, leaned back by `tilt`). The fit keeps them in frame, and a picture of the view — a scene's card, an exported image — has them in it.

The bar at the top of the 3D view drives them: the eye hides or shows the layer; **All** or a deck's chip picks what hangs (⇧-click adds or removes a deck); ‹ and › — or **Page Down** / **Page Up**, what a presenter's clicker sends, and `]` / `[` — step through the slides shown, each time selecting what the slide shows and turning the camera square on to it, with the web and the beams under it; × goes back to the whole picture. A click on a card brings it to the front and selects what it shows (⇧-click adds to the selection) without moving the camera; a double-click also turns the camera to it. A drag that starts on a card still orbits. **＋ Slide** makes a slide of the selection: its title, its deck (or a new one) and its text.

**Composed slides.** A slide can be made of **blocks** instead of lines of text (`data.compose`, a block's id). A block (type `block`) is a reusable piece of a slide: a **flow** — boxes with arrows, `[A] → [B] → [C]` — **stats** (big figures), a **list**, a **timeline** (each box's figure is its date), or **text**; or a **row** or **column** laying other blocks side by side or one under another. Every box is bound to an entity of the graph (`items[].ref`, with a short label, a figure and a note of its own), so a flow on a slide can be a supply chain whose stations are places and organisations in the data, and the numbers on it are the data's. Blocks compose: rows of columns of flows and stats make a slide, and one block — the key numbers, a process — can be used by many slides. The relationships say so too: a block `shows` each entity its boxes are bound to and `composes` each block in it, a slide `composes` its root block, so the inspector, a filter or a script sees what a slide is made of.

In 3D a composed slide's leaf blocks hang beneath it as **plates** of their own — three layers: the web at the bottom, the blocks in the middle, the slides on top. From the region a block takes on the slide a dashed line falls to its plate; from each box of a plate a beam falls to its entity. A block used by several slides hangs once, under the first that uses it, with a line up to every slide that uses it — reuse you can see. Hovering a slide lights the plates it is made of; hovering a plate lights the slides that use it; a click on a plate selects what it shows. The layers button on the bar (beside the eye) folds the plates into their slides, and each box then beams straight from the slide. When the slides first hang over plates, the view is levelled to look at the layers nearly side-on, where they stack without covering one another. Facing a composed slide takes in its plates too.

**Stories: from information to decks, one concern a layer.** A deck can be made from a **story** (type `story`): the directed outline of a document — chapters and, under each, points (type `beat`), in order, each **citing** the entities it draws on. A chapter is realized by a slide (`slide.data.beat`), a point by a leaf block of that slide (`block.data.beat`), so the outline decides what the slides are and what regions they have. With stories the 3D view stacks four layers, each a separate concern:

1. **The web** — the information.
2. **The story** — what was collected from it and in what order: each chapter a card under the left edge of its slide, its points in a row after it, an arrow from every beat to the next in reading order, so the outline runs as one directed path round the ring; from each beat a beam falls to every entity it cites.
3. **The spatial layer** — the outline given geometry: each slide's **blueprint** a level beneath it — the outer rectangle (the slide's frame) and the inner rectangles (the region its composition gives each block, measured from the slide itself), each labelled with the point it realizes, the boxes faint inside them. From each beat a dotted line rises into what realizes it: a chapter to its frame, a point to its rectangle.
4. **The decks** — the finished slides, dotted lines from each blueprint's corners up to its slide's.

Hovering a slide, a beat or a blueprint lights that slide's whole column — its beats, its blueprint, itself; a click on a beat selects what it cites. The bar has a switch for each of the story, the blueprints and the finished slides (the book, the frame and the screen beside the eye), so each layer can be read alone — the story by itself, the story with its geometry, the decks. With stories present the story and the blueprints show unless a scene says otherwise; plates are not hung while they do. Facing a slide takes in its column.

Which decks hang and which slide is in front is part of the view, so a **scene** saves it. Scenes over one shared layout that differ only in their decks are the way to show several projections of one web: switching scenes switches the slides, and the web under them never moves. The slide layer is drawn in 3D only; in 2D the bar and the cards are not shown.

Over MCP: `slides` lists decks, slides and blocks (each block with the slides that use it), reads one with what it shows, makes and changes them (`create` with `members` or `from_selection` or `compose`, `update` with `add` / `remove`, a `plane` or `compose`; its block actions (`slides {create_block}`, `slides {update_block}`) with a `kind` and `items`, `children` or `text`), deletes them (a deck with its slides, after asking), and drives the layer (`view`: `show`, `decks`, `focus` — the slide in front, its members selected and the camera facing it — and `parts`, `story_layer`, `spatial`, `final`); its story actions (`slides {create_story}`, `slides {create_beat}`, `slides {update_beat}`) write stories and beats with what they cite, and `beat` on a slide or block says which beat it realizes. The headless engine's `graph_scene` takes `view.slides` (`show`, `decks`, `focus`, `parts`, `story`, `spatial`, `final`) for scenes composed without a screen.
